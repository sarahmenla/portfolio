Accessible interfaces

Three interfaces, three very different users.

My final-year HCI project: three React web apps, each designed from a research-based persona. A calm learning platform for children aged 5–9, including autistic children; a dental portal for adults over 65; and a fashion shop built keyboard- and screen-reader-first for women with visual impairments.

01

Skills it shows

User research and personas

Each interface starts from a persona built from published research; the learning platform also drew on a survey of four children.

HCI theory in practice

Hick’s and Fitts’s Laws, Nielsen’s heuristics, Gestalt and cognitive load theory, each traced to a design decision.

Accessibility engineering

Keyboard-first: skip links, colours as named radio options, live-region announcements and a spoken keyboard guide.

Testing and evaluation

Task tests with three adult proxies, heuristic reviews, and a WCAG 2.1 audit of the shop.

02

Problem, build, result

1Problem

Most interfaces are built for one imagined user. A child overwhelmed by motion, an older patient lost among buttons and a shopper who cannot see a colour swatch are each shut out in a different way.

2What I built

Three React apps from one research base, applied differently: Calm Mode and untimed games for children; one task per page and Read Aloud for older patients; keyboard-first shopping with named colours.

3Result

Three working interfaces and a First Class grade. In its audit the shop met all seven WCAG 2.1 checks and passed all ten of Nielsen’s heuristics.
